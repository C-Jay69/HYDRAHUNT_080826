# Chapter 1 — Hour Thirty

The lamp had been low since the midnight watch, and now it was lower. Alfred Pym knelt on the cold steel of the strongroom floor and held his breath while the flame settled — blue at the tip, yellow at the base, trembling in a draft that could not exist in a sealed room. He did not let the air out until the trembling stopped. Then he exhaled through his nose, slowly, so as not to waste it. Every breath was a debt against the room's small account, and Pym kept the ledger himself.

He opened the ledger.

The strongroom was a small, stout compartment in the bow, built for specie and ships' papers, not for five breathing bodies. Heavy door. Steel safe. Cold steel at about three degrees Celsius. Above them, the ocean. Below them, the seabed. They had heard the ship go down — the plunge as sound, a roar that filled the steel walls and then cut off — and since then nothing but silence. The silence was not empty. It was full of things pressing in: the weight of two miles of water, the cold, the slow rise of carbon dioxide at floor level where Pym knelt, the smell of lamp oil and unwashed wool and the biscuit tin that had been opened at hour twelve and never properly closed.

Pym ruled a fresh line across the back of a cargo manifest and wrote the figures small and exact.

Hour thirty. Volume of the room: two hundred and forty cubic feet, less the safe, the lantern, the tins of biscuit, the bodies themselves. Five persons at rest. Consumption: roughly half a cubic foot of oxygen per man per hour. The lamp was another mouth, silent and greedy, burning oxygen they could not replace. Each exhalation added carbon dioxide. Each breath made the air fouler, heavier, less useful.

He had done this math at hour six, at hour twelve, at hour twenty-four. Each time the air had lasted longer than his last calculation promised. That was the comfort of arithmetic: it could be redone until it told you what you needed to hear.

This time it did not.

Pym added the column again. He did not round — he never rounded. He added it a second time, and a third. His pencil left a grey track on the paper, and he did not lift it to rub out a figure merely because the figure frightened him. He wrote the numbers with the same care he had used in the purser's office, the same care he would have used if Mr. Lang were still alive to check them.

"The air," he said, "is running out twice as fast as I first calculated."

Mr. Cross did not look up from the safe. He was polishing the dial with a handkerchief, slow and circular, the cloth catching on the edge of the combination ring. "Twice as fast," he said.

"If it's no trouble, sir, I'd like to count again."

"I believe you've counted."

"It's possible that I miscalculated the volume. The safe displaces—"

"The safe displaces what it displaces, Mr. Pym. It does not shrink." Cross tucked the handkerchief into his breast pocket and turned. His smile was rounded and soothing, the kind of smile a man used when he was managing someone through a difficulty he did not intend to share. "Let's not frighten the others yet."

Pym closed his mouth. He opened it. Closed it again. "I will check it a fifth time," he said — flat now, the hedges gone — "and it will say the same."

"Of course you will." Cross stepped closer. He used Pym's name the way he used everyone's name: warm, flowing, as though they were old friends conferring over a pleasant arrangement rather than survivors sealed in a steel box beneath the Atlantic. "We will let the room decide in its own time. In the meantime, Mr. Pym, would you be so good as to keep the figure to yourself? The others are managing."

He apologized in advance, as he always did. "I'm sorry for the burden."

The burden was the ledger. The burden was knowing.

From the far corner, Dai Rees spoke. His voice was concrete, flat, Welsh only in the turn of the sentence. "The seam weeps. Cold, it is. Right along the starboard bulkhead, steady as a clock." He was a stoker; he knew heat and pressure and the behaviour of metal under strain. He narrated physical facts because feelings were helplessness, and helplessness was death. "Lamp's low too. Flame's eating what we breathe. Oxygen, it goes. Carbon dioxide, it stays."

Pym could see the weeping seam in the lamp-light, a dark line running along the plate where the steel had begun to sigh. He did not look at it. He kept his eyes on the ledger.

"Then spare it," said Constance Hale. She did not say please — she never did. "One does wish somebody would do something about that door. One does wish somebody in authority would say whether we are to sit here until the air turns foul, or whether some effort is to be made." A command disguised as an observation, aimed at Cross, who was not in authority and who knew it. "Surely the door is not beyond all hope of opening?"

She sat upright on the bench, her gloves adjusted, her posture formal as though she were still in the first-class lounge awaiting the captain's table. Her armor was cracked, but it had not yet fallen away. Pym could see the crack in the way her hands rested on her lap, too still, too controlled.

Nora Quill stirred on the bench beside her. "Well, that's grand," she said, quick and rhythmic, her Irish in the lift of the sentence rather than the spelling. "Bring out the good silver. Count away, love. It's the only thing keeping you upright." She was the room's morale, and her silence would be the alarm bell. She had not yet fallen silent. Her voice was light, teasing, as though they were waiting in a second-class lounge for tea rather than beneath three thousand metres of water. "And what does the ledger say, Mr. Pym? Has it a kind word for us?"

She reached out and touched Pym's sleeve, briefly, warmly. Her hand was calloused from steward work, from holding trays in a rolling ship, from steadying passengers on a listing deck. She did not pull it away quickly.

The courier stood by the door, his hand on the chained pouch at his belt. Foreign-born, formal, wary. He had spoken once since the sealing. "That is not for opening," he said. One sentence. Clipped.

Pym looked at the pouch, at Cross. Cross's calm logic justified murder, though Pym did not yet know that he knew it. Cross only smiled, warmer and slower now, the way he did when the danger rose. He had not raised his voice. He would not raise his voice.

"Mr. Pym," Cross said. "The tally. Write it down. That is your function."

Pym dipped his pen. He wrote the figure again, small and exact, then drew a single tally mark beside it: one count of air, one hour closer to the end. The ink dried fast in the cold. He did not blow on it. Blowing would waste breath.

"Two hundred and forty cubic feet," he said. "Less the safe. Less the lamp. Less five persons. At hour thirty, the figure stands at approximately thirty-six cubic feet remaining, give or take the lamp's consumption."

"Approximately," Cross said, as though tasting the word and finding it acceptable.

"I do not approximate," Pym said. "I calculate."

"Then calculate on, Mr. Pym. We are all waiting to hear what the room decides."

Rees shifted against the bulkhead. The steel creaked, or perhaps it was only the hull settling on the seabed. "Cold, it is," he said again. "And getting colder. Three degrees, it was. Two now, maybe. The breath shows white in the lamp-light. The hull, it groans. Steel, it talks."

Hale adjusted her gloves, a formal, unnecessary motion. "One does notice the temperature. One does wish somebody would say something about it. Surely a company man such as yourself has some authority over the ambient conditions of a strongroom?"

"I have authority over the safe, Mrs. Hale," Cross said. "And over the pouch. The rest is arithmetic."

Nora laughed. It was a short, bright sound that died quickly in the confined air. "Well, then," she said. "Let the arithmetic decide. Love, pass the biscuit tin. One does get peckish at hour thirty, does one not?"

She was joking, and the joke was dark already, darker than the first hour would have borne. But she had not yet fallen silent. That was the thing to watch. When Nora Quill went silent, things were truly bad.

Cross opened the safe. The heavy door swung on oiled hinges. Inside lay the chained pouch, the manifest, the papers. He touched the pouch once, reverently, as a priest might touch a relic. "The company's interests," he said. "Not for opening. Not until the proper moment."

The courier's hand tightened on his belt. "That is not for opening," he said again.

Cross smiled at him, kind and slow. "Of course not. I was only looking. We are all safe here, are we not? The company looks after its own."

Pym wrote another tally mark. Then another. The ledger was filling up with marks, each one an hour, each one a breath less than there had been before. He did not round the figures. He never rounded. He wrote them down anyway, small and exact, as though precision could keep the dark at bay.

Behind him, in the dark, he remembered the purser's office. Lang's blood on the carpet. Cross's hands, clean. Rudd taking the dead man's coat, the papers, the name. He remembered the sound of the iceberg — not a crash, but a shudder, a long metallic groan that went through the deckplates like a ship sickening. He remembered being sent to the strongroom with the courier and the pouch while Cross stayed behind to check the safe. He remembered the door sealing, the heavy bolt sliding home, the watertight doors closing throughout the bow, and the sea coming in where the iceberg had torn it.

He remembered Lang's last words. They had not been last words at the time, only words, spoken in the purser's office an hour before the iceberg, an hour before Cross killed him. Lang had said the pouch contained the truth of the cargo, the real manifest, the insurance value that did not match the paper. Lang had said he would testify. Lang had said he needed the record kept straight.

Pym had kept the record straight. He had kept it straight through the sinking, through the sealing, through hour thirty and the air running out twice as fast. He had kept it straight because that was his function, because he feared causing a death through a mistake, because he could not bear to round a figure even when rounding would comfort people.

He looked up at Cross. Cross looked back, his face open and concerned, his voice gentle as he said, "Mr. Pym, the tally. Write it down."

Pym wrote it down. He drew another tally mark. The ledger was full of them now, each one an hour, each one a breath less than there had been before. He did not round the figures. He never rounded. He wrote them down anyway, small and exact, as though precision could keep the dark at bay, as though the truth of the number mattered more than the number of breaths they had left.

The steel was three degrees cold against his palm. He wrote the figure again. He drew another mark. Above them, the ocean pressed. Below them, the seabed held. Somewhere between, thirty-six cubic feet of air, give or take a breath — and a company man who had already decided who should be subtracted.

Pym closed the ledger. He did not speak again. He knelt on the cold steel and counted his breaths, one by one, and did not round the number.

Behind him, the others waited. Rees listened to the seam. Hale sat with her hands folded, waiting for somebody in authority to speak. Nora watched Pym with her quick, assessing eye, her joke already fading from her face. Cross stood by the safe, his hand on the pouch, his kindness deepening like a tide coming in. The courier stood by the door, his fingers curled around the chain, his eyes on Cross, his eyes on Pym, his eyes on everything but the darkness above them.

Pym thought of the figure he had not written down: the one that mattered more than any other. Not the volume of the room, not the consumption per man, not the lamp's greedy burn. The figure of how much air a man needed to die peacefully, and how much more to die screaming, and how much less than either they actually had. He had not calculated it because he did not want to know. He did not want to know because Cross already did.

Cross had decided who should be subtracted. Cross was only waiting for the arithmetic to catch up with him.

Pym dipped his pen one last time. He drew a final tally mark beside the last line, a single vertical stroke in the grey dark. He did not round it. He did not soften it. He wrote it down anyway, small and exact, as though the number could still be argued with, as though the ledger might yet speak back.

Above them, the ocean pressed. Below them, the seabed held. Between them, five persons at rest, one company man, one chained pouch, and the air running out twice as fast as it should.

Pym closed the ledger. He did not speak again. He knelt on the cold steel and counted his breaths, one by one, and did not round the number.
