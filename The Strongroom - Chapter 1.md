# Chapter 1 — Hour Thirty

The lamp had burned low since the midnight watch, and now it burned lower. Alfred Pym knelt on the cold steel floor and held his breath while the flame settled—blue at the tip, yellow at the base, trembling in a draft that could not exist in a sealed room. He exhaled through his nose, slowly, so as not to waste it. His hands were cold. His chest felt tight. He told himself it was the carbon dioxide, not fear.

He opened the ledger.

Hour thirty. The strongroom held about four thousand cubic feet of air, less the safe, the lantern, the tins, the six bodies themselves. Six persons at rest. Consumption: roughly half a cubic foot of oxygen per person per hour. The lamp burned oxygen too. Carbon dioxide pooled at the floor, where Pym knelt.

He had done this math at hour six, at hour twelve, at hour twenty-four. Each time the air had lasted longer than his last calculation promised. That was the comfort of arithmetic: it could be redone until it told you what you needed to hear.

This time it did not.

Pym added the column again. He did not round. He added it a second time, then a third. The figures would not change.

"The air," he said, "is running out twice as fast as I first calculated."

Mr. Cross did not look up from the safe. He polished the dial with a handkerchief, slow and circular. "Twice as fast," he said.

"If it's no trouble, sir, I'd like to count again."

"I believe you've counted."

"It's possible that I miscalculated the volume. The safe displaces—"

"The safe displaces what it displaces, Mr. Pym." Cross tucked the handkerchief into his breast pocket and turned. "The room will tell us when it is ready. In the meantime, Mr. Pym, keep the figure to yourself. We are all in this together."

Pym closed his mouth. He opened it. Closed it again.

Cross used his name the way he used everyone's name: warm, flowing, as though they were old friends conferring over a pleasant arrangement. "I'm sorry for the burden, Mr. Pym."

The burden was the ledger. The burden was knowing.

From the far corner, Dai Rees spoke. "The seam weeps. Cold, it is. Right along the starboard bulkhead, steady as a clock." Lamp's low too. Flame's eating what we breathe. Oxygen, it goes. Carbon dioxide, it stays.

"Then spare it," said Constance Hale. "One does wish somebody would do something about that door." She sat upright, gloves adjusted, formal as though she were still awaiting the captain's table.

Nora Quill stirred on the bench beside her. "Well, that's grand," she said, quick and rhythmic. "Bring out the good silver. Count away, love. It's the only thing keeping you upright." She touched Pym's sleeve, briefly, warmly.

The courier stood by the door, his hand on the chained pouch at his belt. He said nothing.

Pym looked at the pouch. He did not know what was inside it. He only knew it was not his to open.

Pym dipped his pen. He wrote the figure again, then drew a single tally mark beside it: one count of air, one hour closer to the end. The ink dried fast in the cold. He did not blow on it.

"The volume is four thousand cubic feet," Pym said. "Less the safe. Less the lamp. Less six persons. At hour thirty, the usable time is roughly half what I first calculated."

"Half," Cross said.

Pym wrote the number down. The steel was cold against his palm. Above them, the water bore down. Below them, the dark was deep. Between them, six persons at rest, one company man, one chained pouch, and the air running out twice as fast as it should.

Pym closed the ledger. He did not speak. He counted his breaths, one by one, on the cold steel.
