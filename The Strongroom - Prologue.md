# Prologue — Fourteen April

The iceberg did not strike the ship like a blow. It sighed against the hull.

Alfred Pym felt it through the sole of his boot: a long, low shudder that ran up the deckplates and stopped. He was sitting on the edge of his bunk in the purser's office, checking the strongroom log against the cargo manifest by lamplight. The ship had been vibrating since Southampton, but this was different. Metal meeting metal at speed. A heavy object parting steel.

Footsteps in the corridor. Mr. Cross appeared in the doorway, sleeves rolled to the forearm. Behind him, the courier, small and dark-coated, his hand on the chain of a leather pouch at his belt.

"The iceberg," Pym said.

"Mr. Ismay says it is nothing," Cross said. "Mr. Lightoller says it is nothing. I say we move the pouch."

"Leave it," Cross said. He stepped aside.

The courier said nothing. He only nodded, once.

Pym counted the watertight doors as they passed through First Class. They stood open. They would not stay open.

The courier stopped at the strongroom door. The heavy steel stood ajar.

"The room," the courier said.

Pym opened it wider. The air inside was cold, about three degrees, the temperature of a compartment built for specie and ships' papers.

The watertight doors began to close throughout the bow, heavy steel sliding home. Nora Quill, Dai Rees and Constance Hale were swept in as the seals engaged. Nora said, quick and rhythmic, "Well, that's grand. Bring out the good silver." Rees pressed his ear to the starboard bulkhead. "The hull," he said. "She's talking." Hale sat upright on the bench, gloves adjusted, formal as though she were still awaiting the captain's table.

Cross entered last. He did not look at Pym. He went to the safe, took the chained pouch from the courier's belt, placed it inside, locked it, and tested the handle.

"The door," Cross said.

Pym threw the bolt. The door swung shut. Cross turned the key. The lock clicked.

They were sealed in.

Above them, the ship groaned again. Not a shudder now — a tearing. Steel parting from steel. Rivets popping. The long, sucking roar of water entering a wounded hull. Pym pressed his hands over his ears. Nora stopped speaking. Rees stepped away from the bulkhead. Hale's hands came together in her lap, too still, too controlled.

The ship went down.

The plunge was sound only: a vast roar that filled the steel walls and then cut off. The strongroom dropped. The lamp flame bent sideways in a draft that could not exist in a sealed room, then went out.

Silence.

Pym knelt on the cold steel floor. He could not see his own hand before his face. He could hear six people breathing. He could hear the seam weeping along the starboard bulkhead, slow and steady. He could hear the ocean above them, holding.

He opened the ledger.

Hour one.

He wrote the figure down. He did not round it.
