# Chapter 2 — The Gala

The Lang Maritime founders' gala filled the museum's central hall. Ines Calloway kept her glass half-full so that she would not have to accept a second one from a waiter. She stood beneath the bronze statue of Theodore Lang—life-sized, one hand resting on a ship's railing, the other holding an invisible hat to his brow in a gesture of modest acknowledgment. The plaque read: SURVIVOR. VISIONARY. FOUNDER. 1881–1962.

She noted the death date. She noted that no photograph of Lang as a young man appeared in the ground-floor display. There were images of the ship, of the iceberg field, of the Carpathia's approach at dawn, of lifeboats clustered in the dark like scattered walnut shells. Not of him. Not in 1912. Not in anything earlier than 1915, when Lang Maritime had been founded.

She wrote it down in her notebook.

Shipping executives, museum trustees, insurers from Lloyd's, a few members of the family office filled the room. A string quartet played in the corner, low and unobtrusive. Waiters moved through the crowd with trays of champagne and canapés. The lighting was warm and low, the kind intended to make people feel generous. Ines kept her back to the wall and her notebook open. She was here for her book, a working title she had not yet decided to keep, and she was here because the Lang story had a hole in it that no amount of corporate polishing could fill.

A man near the bar laughed too loudly. A woman in a blue dress adjusted her necklace. Ines watched them without seeing them. Her attention remained on the statue, on the plaque, on the gap between the legend and the record.

Her phone buzzed once in her pocket. She did not look at it.

Across the room, a man in a dark suit spoke to a small cluster near the bar. Grey hair, carefully parted, a tie that matched the navy of the Lang Maritime livery. He held a champagne flute he had not touched. Ines watched him for a moment, then turned back to her notes.

Boarding record, Theodore Lang, first class, ticket 13052, embarked Southampton, age 31, occupation investor, next of kin none listed, address c/o IMM New York.

She had found that in the Lloyd's archive three days ago. Clean, complete, unimpeachable. The kind of record that satisfied casual inquiry. But when she had traced Lang's handwriting through the company's early correspondence—the letters to shipbuilders, the insurance claims, the safety reports—the script shifted after 1912. Before the sinking, the hand was quick, forward-leaning, the t's crossed with a single sharp stroke. After, it became rounded, deliberate, as though the writer had learned to form each letter with care.

Ines had not yet written that down. She was saving it.

A waiter passed with a tray. Ines declined a glass with a small lift of her chin.

The man in the navy tie moved toward her. He stopped two paces away, holding his flute at waist level, and smiled. It was a rounded smile, the kind that did not reach the eyes but made an effort to suggest they might.

"Ms. Calloway," he said.

"Mr. —?"

"Ashcombe. Julian Ashcombe. I represent the Lang family office." He extended a hand. "I believe there is a mutual interest in the accuracy of the record."

Ines took his hand. His grip was dry and firm, his palm cool. "I represent no one but myself, Mr. Ashcombe. And my publisher."

"Of course. The book." He inclined his head, a small, precise nod. "We have followed your work with interest. The piece on the Hawke ruling was particularly thorough. Gratitude is always due when the historical record is attended to."

"It's not the historical record I'm attending to tonight," Ines said. "It's the hospitality."

"A fair distinction." Ashcombe glanced at the statue. "Mr. Lang cut a fine figure. One does not object to his being remembered."

"No. One does not."

"But one does wonder, sometimes, whether the memory serves the man or the institution." Ashcombe took a sip of his champagne. He did not lower the glass all the way. "Lang Maritime is the safest line in the industry. That is not a claim made lightly. It is a record built on the lessons of 1912."

"On one man's knowledge of what went wrong," Ines said.

"On one man's survival," Ashcombe said. "There is a difference."

Ines said nothing. She opened her notebook and wrote: Ashcombe, Julian. Family office. Passive voice. No contractions.

She looked up. "Is that your line, Mr. Ashcombe? Or the family office's?"

"It is the family office's line. I am merely the voice." He used her name then—Ms. Calloway—as though testing its weight. "You are writing about the survivor. The family office is the keeper of his legacy. Interests are not opposed."

"Are they not?"

"The truth is wanted by all." Ashcombe's voice remained level, flat, uninflected. "What should be done with it is where views differ."

Before Ines could answer, a waiter appeared at her elbow with a silver tray. On it sat a single white envelope, unmarked, sealed with wax the color of old bone.

"For you, madam," the waiter said.

Ines took it. The envelope was heavy, the paper thick. There was no name, no return address, no indication of how it had entered the building or who had delivered it. She turned it over. Nothing on the back.

"Thank you," she said.

The waiter had already moved on.

Ashcombe watched her, his expression unchanged. "An anonymous communication," he said. "How intriguing."

"Or how tiresome," Ines said. She broke the wax seal. Inside was a single sheet of paper, folded once. No letterhead. No signature. Just one page, typed in a font that looked like it had come from an old office machine.

She unfolded it and read.

Lifeboat Register, Boat 13, 15 April 1912. Entry: T. Lang, first class. The signature on that line appears in ink consistent with post-1915 manufacture. Verification requested.

Ines read it twice. She read it a third time. Her thumb rested on the paper, her pulse steady in her wrist.

She folded it carefully and slipped it into her notebook.

"You are quiet," Ashcombe said. "I assume you will not open that here."

"It's a lifeboat register anomaly," Ines said. "One page. No context."

"A curious thing to receive at a gala," Ashcombe said. "Though curiosity is not the same as significance. Many such things are received. Families with grievances, journalists with theories, people who believe they have found the flaw in an otherwise sound record."

"And what do you do with them?"

"They are filed," Ashcombe said. "Receipt is acknowledged. The record remains undisturbed." He paused. "I apologize in advance, Ms. Calloway, if my curiosity seems intrusive. But I wonder—did the envelope arrive with any accompanying explanation?"

"None."

"Then there is agreement." Ashcombe smiled, the rounded, soothing smile again. "The record stands as it stands. One page does not change a legacy."

"No," Ines said. "But it does raise a question."

"Does it?"

"Whether the signature on Boat 13 belongs to the man whose statue stands in this lobby."

Ashcombe's expression did not change. He took another sip of champagne. "Handwriting varies with age, with injury, with the instrument used. A fountain pen in 1912 is not a ballpoint in 1920. A man signing his name in a lifeboat, in the dark, after a shipwreck—one does not expect his finest calligraphy."

"No," Ines said. "One does not."

"You have found something, then," Ashcombe said. It was not a question. It was an observation, phrased so that she could confirm or deny it without giving anything away. "A discrepancy. An anomaly."

"I've found a page of paper," Ines said. "That's all."

"Of course." Ashcombe nodded. "And yet you are holding it as though it weighs more than paper."

Ines said nothing. She placed the envelope back in her notebook, closed the cover, and tucked the notebook under her arm.

"The exhibition is upstairs," Ashcombe said. "The founders' museum. Mr. Lang's personal effects. The lifeboat oar he was said to have carried. The letter he wrote to his sister from the Carpathia. You might find it illuminating. Or you might not. One never knows, with personal effects."

"I'll look," Ines said.

"Of course you will." Ashcombe stepped back, giving her room to pass. "The record will speak in its own time. In the meantime, Ms. Calloway, would you be so good as to keep the page to yourself?"

He said it softly. Kindly. As though they were old friends conferring over a pleasant arrangement.

Ines looked at him. She did not smile. "The page."

"Precisely."

Ashcombe turned and moved back toward the cluster near the bar. He did not look back. He did not need to.

Ines opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote:

Ashcombe, Julian. Family office. Passive voice. No contractions. Warns me without threatening. Tells me to keep the page to herself.

She closed the notebook and looked at the statue. Theodore Lang, survivor, visionary, founder. One hand on the railing, the other holding an invisible hat. The plaque did not explain what it meant to survive. It did not explain what it meant to found.

Ines turned and walked toward the museum. Her heels clicked on the marble, steady, exact, one step after another. She counted them.

The founders' museum occupied a single long gallery. Glass cases lined the walls, each labeled in the same navy typeface as Ashcombe's tie. A lifebuoy from a Lang ship hung on the far wall, painted white with red lettering. A model of a cargo vessel sat on a plinth in the center of the room, its tiny decks detailed with miniature railings and lifeboats. A display case held a yellowed newspaper, the headlines faded but still legible: TITANIC SURVIVOR FOUNDS SHIPPING LINE. Beneath it, a smaller case held a pocket watch, its face cracked, its hands stopped at twenty minutes past eleven. The label read: WATCH, RECOVERED FROM THE WRECK, 1985. ATTRIBUTED TO THEODORE LANG. Ines looked at the watch. She thought about the hands stopped at twenty minutes past eleven. She thought about the iceberg strike at twenty minutes before midnight. She wrote nothing down.

The lifeboat oar rested in a case of its own, a plain piece of wood with a leather-wrapped handle. The label said: OAR, LIFEBOAT 13, TITANIC, 1912. DONATED BY THEODORE LANG, 1915. Ines looked at the oar. She looked at the label. She wrote nothing down.

The letter from the Carpathia lay in another case, enclosed in glass, its ink faded to brown. The label said: LETTER FROM THEODORE LANG TO HIS SISTER, ABOARD THE CARPATHIA, 15 APRIL 1912. Ines leaned closer. She could not read the handwriting from this distance. The glass reflected the overhead lights.

She stepped back. She pulled out her notebook. She wrote: Museum exhibits. Oar from Boat 13. Letter from Carpathia. Watch recovered from wreck. All attributed to Lang. None verified.

A fourth case held a life vest, canvas and cork, faded to grey. The label read: LIFE VEST, LIFEBOAT 13, TITANIC, 1912. WORN BY THEODORE LANG. Ines looked at the life vest. She thought about the capacity of Boat 13. She thought about the number of people who should have been in it. She thought about the number who were. She wrote nothing down.

A fifth case held a telegram, yellowed at the edges, its text typewritten in neat rows. The label read: TELEGRAM, FROM THEODORE LANG, TO LANG MARITIME HEAD OFFICE, 16 APRIL 1912. Ines leaned closer. She could read the words: Safe. Aboard Carpathia. Sending full report. Ines stepped back. She wrote nothing down.

A sixth case held a silver cigarette case, engraved with initials. The label read: CASE, OWNED BY THEODORE LANG. PRESENTED TO HIM BY WHITE STAR LINE, 1908. Ines looked at the initials. T.L. She thought about the date — 1908, four years before the sinking. She thought about the watch stopped at twenty minutes past eleven. She wrote nothing down.

She turned toward the exit. A guard stood by the door, his hands behind his back, his eyes on the room. Ines nodded to him. He did not nod back.

She turned toward the exit. A guard stood by the door, his hands behind his back, his eyes on the room. Ines nodded to him. He did not nod back.

She walked back to the central hall. The gala continued around her. The string quartet played on. The waiters moved through the crowd. The statue of Theodore Lang stood at the far end, bronze, life-sized, one hand on a ship's railing, the other holding an invisible hat to his brow.

Ines did not look at it. She walked toward the exit. Her heels clicked on the marble, measured and even, one step after another. She counted them.

She pushed through the doors. The night air struck her face, cool and sudden. She stood on the steps for a moment, her notebook under her arm, the anonymous page inside it.

She hailed a taxi. When it arrived, she got in the back seat, closed the door, and gave the driver her address. She watched the museum recede in the rearview mirror, the lights of the central hall glowing behind the glass doors.

She opened her notebook. She wrote:

Lang, Theodore. Boarding record clean. Handwriting changes after 1912. Boat 13 entry questioned by an anonymous page. Cannot confirm identity. Cannot confirm fraud. Can only confirm absence.

She underlined absence once. Then twice.

She turned away from the window. She sat at the table. She opened her notebook. She picked up her pen. She wrote:

Next steps. Find the sender. Verify the signature. Check the Carpathia passenger list. Contact Lloyd's again. Ask about the early life records.

She underlined verification once. Then twice.

She closed the notebook. She did not round anything.

She made her decision. She would follow the register lead.
